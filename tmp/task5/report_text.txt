02 / BUSINESS MODEL
Choose the monetization archetype
Beachhead and transaction roles
The first paying customer is the owner or administrator of a one-location flower, gift or small social-commerce
shop in Almaty. The base customer makes 40 repeat deliveries a month, carried forward as an assumption from
Task 4. Private senders can use Qadam but are excluded from this business-customer acquisition model. [T4]
Demand side: shops needing local parcel delivery. Supply side: couriers accepting and completing orders. The
recipient receives the parcel and status information. Qadam coordinates order creation, acceptance, handoff,
status history and last-known location.
Archetype
Assessment and selection
Subscription / SaaS
Predictable billing, but a mandatory fee before delivery demand is proven raises
adoption friction. Stable usage and low churn have not been demonstrated. Not the
primary model.
Marketplace percentage rake
Would scale with courier fare, including distance and traffic that do not necessarily
increase coordination value. A deduction from courier earnings would change the 0%
pilot proposition. Rejected for the first commercial test.
Transaction / usage-based
Selected. A fixed customer-side fee per completed delivery aligns revenue with useful
outcomes, is easy to audit and lets small shops start without a commitment.
Freemium / hybrid
An indefinite free tier would incur support costs before any demonstrated 5-10%
premium conversion. Pro and Enterprise use minimum usage commitments, fully
credited to delivery fees, rather than an extra subscription.
Transaction and money flow
1. The shop creates an order and sees courier fare and platform fee separately.
2. A courier accepts, collects and completes the delivery with a completion record.
3. The agreed courier fare is paid directly to the courier. Qadam invoices the shop for completed-delivery fees
monthly. No platform fee is charged for cancellations or unmatched orders.
Start with local liquidity
A proposed test cluster is 10 shops in one service zone: 10 x 40 / 26 = 15.4 deliveries per dispatch day. At a
hypothetical peak of five requests/hour, a 45-minute full courier cycle and 70% planned utilization, required online
supply is ceil(5 x 45 / 60 / 0.70) = 6 couriers, plus two roster backups. These are capacity assumptions, not
registered or committed supply.
Commercial test gates: at least 90% of valid requests accepted within 10 minutes and at least 95% of accepted orders completed.
Recheck them by zone and time window before expanding. Marketplace match rate and time to match are distinct from courier
utilization. [A1]

The assignment's 10x requirement remains unvalidated
A genuine 10x active-time result would require 600 / 60 = 10x. The 60-second workflow is an engineering
target. At that target, released labor value would be KZT 225/order. The financial base case uses the earlier
90-second assumption, so the 10x target does not inflate LTV.
Keep the economic claim precise
Including an illustrative, unchanged KZT 1,500 courier fare, full cost per order is KZT 1,750 As-Is and KZT
1,657.50 To-Be including the fee. That is about 5.3% total-cost reduction, not 10x cheaper delivery. Released
staff capacity becomes cash savings only if the shop actually reduces paid labor or uses the time to generate
additional contribution.

04 / BUSINESS MODEL
Price against value and switching costs
Why cost plus 20% fails
It ignores willingness to pay. A shop evaluates less administrative work, reliable coordination and alternatives.
Server costs do not reveal what that shop will pay. Under the base cost assumptions, non-payment service cost
is KZT 25/order plus KZT 600/40 in account costs, or KZT 40/order. Adding 20% gives KZT 48 before payment
costs. This does not test whether the customer would accept the proposed KZT 120, or reject any fee at all.
It ignores switching costs. Moving from WhatsApp and a known courier requires setup, training and trust. Even
a price below estimated savings may fail when a shop has an efficient existing workflow or unreliable courier
supply. A 30-minute setup at KZT 1,500/hour is an assumed KZT 750 one-time cost. It belongs in the
customer's adoption decision, separately from Qadam's onboarding expense in CAC.
It can leave value uncaptured or overprice a weak product. A percentage markup treats low technical cost as
low value and may push prices up when the platform is inefficient. A 20% markup is only a 16.7% margin before
payment costs. Qadam should use costs to test feasibility, then test the customer's economic benefit and actual
WTP to set the fee.
One primary value metric
A completed delivery coordinated for a paying shop
A billable event is an order with a retained completed-delivery record. Each completed order is charged once.
Cancellation, duplicate retries and unmatched requests do not create platform-fee events. Fees for failed
attempts are not added silently.
This unit scales with the shop's number of fulfilled dispatches. Each additional order creates a coordination task
and, under the time hypothesis, another KZT 212.50 of released labor capacity. Seats penalize a shop for adding
staff. Distance and parcel value can increase courier compensation without increasing coordination value, so
neither is the primary platform pricing unit.
Starter value capture and adoption hurdle
KZT 120 captures 56.5% of the modeled KZT 212.50/order benefit, leaving KZT 92.50/order with the shop. At 40
orders, first-month net capacity value after setup is 8,500 - 4,800 - 750 = KZT 2,950. Recovering the setup effort
takes 750 / 92.50 = 8.1 completed orders under the assumptions.
The retained value is a pricing hypothesis, not proof of WTP. Validate paid acceptance and repeat use at the
proposed price. If realized savings are lower, revise the price, scope or segment rather than assuming interviews
guarantee purchase.

Transparent billing rules
Monthly platform bill = fee x max(actual completed orders, committed order equivalents). The commitment is
fully credited against usage. A Pro customer with 120 completed orders pays 120 x 90 = KZT 10,800, not KZT
19,800. Starter with 40 orders pays KZT 4,800.
At the minimum commitments, modeled monthly labor benefit is KZT 21,250 for Pro and KZT 106,250 for
Enterprise. The respective benefits retained after platform fees are KZT 12,250 and KZT 76,250. A shop should
commit only when its expected usage makes the plan economical. Unused commitments expire monthly and
must be explained before purchase.
Scope follows delivery capability
Existing core coordination is documented in the project. Batch tools, commercial reporting, custom integrations,
dedicated SLAs and enhanced enterprise controls are proposed capabilities. Enterprise KZT 60 is a planning
floor for an agreed scope. Custom development and support must be costed before a quote is accepted. This
report does not estimate enterprise LTV from Starter economics.
Any Enterprise SLA should specify measurable service availability and support-response obligations after testing. It must not
promise continuous courier GPS when the Mini App is closed or guaranteed delivery timing without validated supply. The courier
fare remains separate in every tier. Prices are modeled before applicable taxes; no tax registration or tax rate is assumed.

Gross-profit lifetime value
 Expected lifetime = 1 / c = 1 / 0.03 = 33.33 months.
LTV = R x g / c = GP / c = 3,104 / 0.03 = KZT 103,467.
LTV / CAC = 103,466.67 / 24,000 = 4.31x.
LTV measures lifetime gross profit, not lifetime revenue or courier GMV. It assumes constant monthly churn,
constant order volume and margin, no expansion, no reactivation and no discounting. A 3% monthly churn
assumption implies (1 - 0.03)12 = 69.4% twelve-month logo retention. This is unobserved and materially affects
LTV.
CAC payback
 Simple payback = CAC / monthly GP = 24,000 / 3,104 = 7.73 months.
The monthly cohort also applies churn between months. Expected survival in month t is (1 - c)t-1. The first month
with cumulative expected gross profit at least KZT 24,000 is month 9. This survival-adjusted payback is more
conservative than the simple ratio.
36-month capped check
 LTV36 = GP x [1 - (1 - c)36] / c
= 3,104 x [1 - 0.9736] / 0.03 = KZT 68,906.
LTV36 / CAC = 68,905.94 / 24,000 = 2.87x.
The workbook sums 36 monthly retained-store contributions, so zero churn still produces a finite capped value.
Uncapped LTV is shown as unavailable at zero churn instead of reporting an infinite figure. No residual value is
added after month 36.

What must improve
At base gross profit and CAC, uncapped LTV/CAC reaches 3x only if monthly churn is at most 4.31%. Simple

To reach 3x on capped LTV, CAC must fall to KZT 22,969 or less. At the same acquisition budget, 21 paying
stores/month would produce CAC of KZT 22,857. Alternatively, about 42 orders/store/month would cross the
capped hurdle at unchanged CAC and churn. These are testable thresholds, not achieved results.
With KZT 120,000 fixed operating overhead plus the KZT 480,000 acquisition pool, indicative operating
breakeven is ceil(600,000 / 3,104) = 194 active Starter stores. This excludes tax, capital expenditure and
working-capital timing. Positive unit economics alone does not establish cash breakeven.

Risks that change the model
Low usage or seasonality: fewer orders reduce margin per account and raise payback. Supply gaps: credits,
support and courier incentives may exceed the allowance. Off-platform repeat orders: shops and couriers can
bypass fees unless history, convenience and service deliver enough recurring value.
Billing and collection: monthly invoicing creates receivables; LTV is an economic estimate, not a cash-flow
schedule. Define payment terms and separately measure late payment and bad debt. Product scope: custom
integrations and SLAs can create new fixed support costs and must not inherit Starter economics without a
separate build.
Proceed in stages
Use this assignment to design the commercial test. Charging fees, activating tariffs, changing courier terms
and implementing paid-tier features each require the project owner's approval. The current report and
workbook do not change the operating pilot.
